I. — through the year, in four walks

Seasonal field entries

Four dates I would press like flowers if I could. Each entry was written sitting down — on a stump, a stone, a root, a frozen gate.

23 March — Vernal Pool8°C · drizzle

Spring frog-song & first green

The wood sorrel unclenches. I counted eleven frog clumps in the black water, each like a handful of glass marbles. A treecreeper stitched the oak trunk in spirals, never using the same bark twice.

✿ wood anemone — abundant
19 July — Hay Meadow Edge24°C · thunder near

Summer hay-sweet & hoverflies

Heat held under the pines like breath under wool. Chanterelles pushed through needle-duff in apricot ruffles. I lay flat to watch a stag beetle right a fallen comrade — antler to antler, solemn as wrestlers.

☀ meadow brown — 40+ on the wing
14 October — Old Oak Fall11°C · mist

Autumn inkcaps & jay-shrieks

The big oak dropped a limb in the night — clean break, white wood showing like bone. By morning it was already a village: inkcaps, springtails, a jay caching acorns in the wound. Death, dealt with briskly.

🍄 fly agaric — first flush
02 February — Frozen Weir-3°C · clear

Winter wren under ice-song

The stream spoke in a lower register, under glass. Every alder cone wore a snow-cap, tipped sideways like a drunk’s hat. A dipper blinked white eyelids at me and carried on — the only one of us still earning a living.

⌕ How to read it

  • Old Oak Wood — tawny owls, lichens, deep leaf-mould. Quietest at noon.
  • Hay Meadow — orchids in June, voles always. Ask farmer before crossing.
  • Blackwater — dippers, caddis, kingfisher every third visit if you’re lucky.
  • Bog — sundew + snipe. Do not chase hat if wind takes it.
  • Fox path — my loop. Stiles marked with pocket-knife nicks.

IV. — the one I keep re-reading

The night the fallen oak breathed

14 – 15 October · Old Oak Wood · mist, moon at ¾, tawny owls 2 · written by torchlight, pencil smudged by breath.

I went back at ten to listen to a tree die properly. The limb the storm took that morning lay with its leaves still green and shocked, as if it hadn’t been told. I expected silence. Instead the wood was — I have no better word — busy.

First the owls. One hoo… ho-hooo from the standing trunk, answered lower from the quarry. Then a sound I first took for rain: thousands of tiny tickings as acorns let go in the cooling air and struck leaf-litter, each with its own small decision of bounce or settle. I counted twenty-seven in one minute and then gave up counting, which felt like the correct scientific method.

“Decay is not an ending. It is a change of landlord.” — margin, 11:04 p.m.

By midnight the mist had come up to my knees. I switched off the torch and let my eyes widen. Fungi I’d missed by day showed as pale commas along the wound-wood. A wood mouse ran the length of the fallen limb like a commuter who knew the timetable, pausing only to wash its face in moonlight. Somewhere upstream, a kingfisher — impossibly — gave its thin peee in the dark, as if it too couldn’t sleep for the excitement of rot.

I put my palm on the break. The wood was warm. Not metaphorically — truly a degree or two above the air, where yeasts and beetles and mycelium were already at work, unmaking oak into soil into oak again. I thought of all my neat specimen boxes and felt gently rebuked. The forest does not collect. It continues.

I walked home at one, damp to the thigh, with ink on my cheek and a jay feather in my hat. Behind me the owls kept calling, stitching the dark together so it wouldn’t tear where the branch had gone.

Method: sat still 2 hrs 40 min. No specimens taken. Crumbs left for mouse as collaborator’s fee. See weather ledger: Oct 14 · pressure falling, wind SW 3.

Field courtesies ✎

Close gates. Step over, not on, sphagnum. No picking orchids. If you turn a stone, put it back as you found its damp side down — someone lives under there.

→ leave only footprints, take only notes (+ one feather if freely given)

VII. — who scribbles here

About the naturalist

Hello — I’m Eleanor (“Wren”) Hart, amateur naturalist, volunteer warden, and professional sit-still-and-watcher. For twelve years I’ve walked the same five miles of Blackwater wood in all weathers, learning its regulars by footfall and feather.

I’m not a scientist by trade (I mend books), but I record like one: dates, weather, measurements, mistakes crossed out legibly so future-me can laugh. My rule is small, slow, often — ten minutes noticing beats a whole hurried hike.

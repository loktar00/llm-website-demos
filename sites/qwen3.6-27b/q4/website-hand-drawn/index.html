The Moss Notebook

a field journal · one year in the old growth

Four seasons of slow watching, pressed specimens, and the quiet grammar of forests — recorded in pencil, ink, and whatever light the canopy would give.

Seasonal Entries

four chapters, written as the light changed

Spring

March – May

The snow melts into the creek and the forest floor wakes in patches. First wood anemones push through the leaf litter. I found a nest of frog eggs in the hollow log — a jelly of tiny dark seeds. The air smells of wet bark and possibility.

Summer

June – August

Canopy closes overhead. The light becomes green-gold and thick. Cicadas begin their sawing in July. I spent three afternoons watching a red-tailed hawk circle the same thermal above the meadow. Mushrooms bloom overnight after the first real rain.

Autumn

September – November

The maples go first — a slow burn from the ridge down. I pressed fifty leaves this season, each one a different shade of reluctant surrender. The mushrooms arrive in earnest: chanterelles, hen-of-the-woods, the ghostly oyster caps on dead hemlock.

Winter

December – February

Silence, mostly. The creek narrows to a thread. I track a deer family through the frozen meadow — their prints like small commas in the snow. The lichen on the north-facing rocks glows pale green in the low sun. Waiting, the forest teaches me, is not passive.

Specimen Notes

seven things collected, pressed, and pondered

No. 001 · collected April 12

Wood Fern

Dryopteris expansa

Found along the creek bank, growing in dense clumps. Fronds uncurl like fiddleheads in early spring. The underside bears neat rows of brown sori — tiny spore factories.

No. 002 · collected October 3

Chanterelle

Cantharellus cibarius

Golden trumpet shape, firm flesh with apricot scent. Gills run down the stem like fluting. Found under beech trees in late October — the forest's hidden feast.

No. 003 · collected March 28

Robin Egg

Turdus migratorius

Found abandoned in a dead branch after a storm. The blue is impossibly bright — like holding a piece of sky. Spotted with faint rust near the blunt end. Returned to the branch after measuring.

No. 004 · collected November 15

White Pine Needle

Pinus strobus

Five needles in a fascicle, soft and flexible — the white pine's signature. Each bundle lasts two years on the branch. The resin scent lingers on my fingers for hours.

No. 005 · collected July 22

Seven-Spot Ladybird

Coccinella septempunctata

Seven spots, exactly, on each wing case. Found on a rose bush, legs tucked in a perfect sphere when disturbed. A tiny armored knight patrolling the garden.

No. 006 · collected September 8

Barred Owl Feather

Strix varia

Found on the forest floor, primary flight feather. The barring is precise — bands of grey and buff like tree rings. The rachis is strong, slightly curved. The owl sheds these in the hollow of a dead pine.

No. 007 · collected October 20

White Oak Acorn

Quercus alba

Smooth, pale brown nut in a deeply cupped cap. The cap's scales are rough like tiny shingles. A jay has buried dozens within a meter of my boots — I leave them be.

The Heron at Dusk

June 14 · 19:47 · by the lower pool, 43.2°N

It stood in the shallows for nearly an hour, a grey column against the gold light. A great blue heron — I'd seen them before, but never patient enough to watch one work.

The technique is almost meditative. One foot lifts, inches at a time, the body tilting forward with the grace of a bowing dancer. The beak is a spear, but the strike is so fast it's invisible — just a ripple, a flash of silver, and the heron swallows whole.

Three fish in forty minutes. A kingfisher challenged it once, diving from the alder branch, but the heron didn't even turn. It had the water's rhythm and would not be hurried.

When it finally flew, the wings made a sound like pages turning — wide, slow, deliberate. It headed north along the creek, and I sat on the log until the light went green, then grey, then nothing.

Note: the heron returned the next evening to the same spot. Some creatures keep better appointments than humans.

Soundscape

what the forest sounds like, month by month

Spring Chorus

Wood thrushes fluting from the hemlock understory, chorus frogs clicking in the marsh, the creek swelling with snowmelt. A layered, overlapping music — no single voice dominates.

Apr–May

Midnight Cicadas

A sawing, electric hum that rises and falls in waves. The sound is so thick it feels like pressure. It never stops — it only breathes. The forest's own metronome.

Jul–Aug

Autumn Wind

Dry leaves skittering across the forest floor like small animals. The wind moves through the canopy in long sighs. A red fox calls once at dusk — a high, yipping bark that answers nothing.

Sep–Oct

Winter Silence

Not true silence — the creek still whispers under ice, wind still moves through bare branches. But the sound is thin, transparent. A single crow's call carries for a mile. The forest holds its breath.

About the Naturalist

I am a person who walks slowly. This notebook is my way of paying attention — of slowing down enough to notice the grammar of small things: how a fern uncurls, how light moves through water, how a mushroom appears overnight like a decision the forest has made.

I've walked this watershed for twelve years now. I know which log holds the chanterelles, which branch the heron prefers, which rocks the lichen has claimed. The forest has taught me patience, which is to say it has taught me to stop wanting things to happen faster.

Every entry here is written in the field, in pencil, before being inked at home. The drawings are mine — imperfect, I know, but honest. A pressed leaf is worth more than a photograph because it carries the weight of the moment it was found.

This is not a scientific record. It is a love letter, written one season at a time.

— E. Whitfield

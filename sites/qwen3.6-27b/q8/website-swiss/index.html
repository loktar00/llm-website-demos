Our holdings span printed maps, wayfinding systems, fare media, station clocks, and digital interfaces — each object a testament to the clarity and precision required to guide millions of daily movements.

First Underground Railway Opens
The Metropolitan Railway opens in London, the world's first underground electric railway. Early maps are geographical, showing streets and landmarks alongside the tunnel route.
Berlin U-Bahn Opens
The Hochbahn opens in Berlin, introducing the first systematic approach to station signage with standardized typography and directional indicators.
Harry Beck's Diagrammatic Map
Electrical engineer Harry Beck publishes his revolutionary topological map of the London Underground, replacing geographical accuracy with a grid-based schematic that transforms transit mapping forever.
Wayfinding Systems Emerge
Post-war reconstruction prompts systematic approaches to station wayfinding. Color-coded lines, standardized pictograms, and hierarchical typography become industry standards.
Helvetica Adopted by Swiss Railways
The SBB/CFF/FFS commissions Max Miedinger and Herbert Matter to develop a unified corporate identity for Swiss Federal Railways, establishing Helvetica as the voice of transit clarity.
International Pictogram Standards
ISO 7001 establishes international standards for public information symbols. Transit systems worldwide adopt unified pictograms for elevators, restrooms, exits, and transfers.
Tokyo Metro's Universal Design
Tokyo Metro implements a comprehensive multilingual wayfinding system combining Latin, Japanese, and pictographic elements — a model for complex multilingual transit environments.
Archive Founded
The Helvetica Transit Archive is established in Zürich, beginning systematic collection and documentation of transit information design objects from around the world.
Digital Transformation
Real-time digital displays replace static information boards. Mobile applications and contactless payment systems begin reshaping the passenger information ecosystem.
The Archive Today
With over 14,000 objects spanning 160 years, the archive continues to document the evolving visual language of public transportation — now extending into augmented reality and AI-assisted navigation.

Featured Essay

Dieter Kuhn
Swiss designer (1930–2019)
SBB/CFF/FFS Corporate Identity
Zürich S-Bahn Map System

The clarity of a transit map is not an accident of design but the result of relentless reduction. Dieter Kuhn understood this better than perhaps any designer of his generation. When he took on the Zürich S-Bahn map in 1989, he was not merely drawing lines on paper — he was solving the problem of how to make a city legible to someone who has never seen it.

Kuhn's approach was characteristically Swiss: mathematical, unsentimental, and deeply humane. He reduced the network to its essential topology, eliminating geographic distortion while preserving the spatial relationships that passengers actually need. The result was a map that could be read in three seconds — a critical metric when you are standing on a platform with a train arriving in forty-five seconds.

"A transit map is not a map. It is a diagram of decisions. Every line, every color, every angle exists to help a stranger choose the correct path."

The archive holds 347 objects from Kuhn's working process for the S-Bahn identity: preliminary sketches, color studies, type specimens, and the final production files. What emerges is a portrait of a designer who believed that public information design was the most important work a designer could undertake — because it serves everyone, equally, without distinction.

His influence extends far beyond Zürich. The principles he developed — grid-based alignment, restrained color palettes, hierarchical typography, and the elimination of decorative elements — became the template for transit information design across Europe and eventually the world. When you hold a transit map in any major city today, you are holding the descendant of Kuhn's work.

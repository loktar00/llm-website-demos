FIELD NOTES VOL. VII

Seasonal Observations

Four turns of the wheel

Spring
March 20 — June 20

The forest floor awakens in soft greens. Morel mushrooms push through last autumn's leaf litter. Wood warblers return with their frantic, seed-like songs. The stream swells with snowmelt, running brown and eager.

→ first trillium spotted April 3rd, near the cedar grove
Summer
June 21 — September 22

Canopy closes overhead. Humidity wraps the trails in a warm blanket. Dragonflies patrol the pond like tiny helicopters. The air hums with cicadas at dusk. Ferns unfurl their fiddleheads into full, lacey fronds.

→ hottest day: July 18, 94°F. Sat by the stream all afternoon.
Autumn
September 23 — December 21

The maples ignite. Jack-o'-lantern mushrooms glow faintly in the twilight. Pileated woodpeckers drum their unmistakable rhythm. The forest floor becomes a mosaic of russet and gold. First frost silvers the spiderwebs.

→ collected 14 leaf specimens before the big wind
Winter
December 22 — March 19

Snow mutes all sound. The world becomes a study in white and gray. Pine branches bow under their burden. Tracks in the snow tell stories: fox, deer, the occasional raccoon. Bare branches reveal the forest's skeleton.

→ ice fishing at Miller's Pond. Caught nothing but silence.

The Salamander Crossing

March 14, 2024 · 6:42 AM

The rain had just stopped. The air smelled of wet stone and turned earth — that particular perfume of early spring when the ground is still thawing, still remembering winter. I was walking the lower trail, the one that follows Cedar Creek, when I saw it: a line of small, dark shapes moving across the muddy path.

Spotted salamanders. Dozens of them. Perhaps a hundred. Moving with that strange, determined waddle from the saturated woods toward the vernal pool half a mile downstream. They emerge like this only a few nights a year, during the brief window when the soil is warm enough and the rain has made the crossing possible.

"They move with the urgency of creatures who know the window is narrow. Each one a small, black arrow pointing toward the water."

I knelt beside the path and watched them pass. Their skin glistened in the flat morning light — jet black with yellow spots, the spots arranged in two irregular rows down each side. Some were large, nearly six inches. Others were smaller, perhaps in their first breeding migration.

A few had already reached the creek's edge and were working their way down the bank, slipping between wet leaves and stones. One paused at my boot, considered it for a long moment, then continued on its way as if my presence were merely another obstacle in the landscape.

I sat there for nearly an hour, watching them cross. A deer stepped carefully around the procession. A red-tailed hawk circled overhead but seemed uninterested — these creatures are too small, too slow, too deeply committed to their ancient migration to be worth the effort.

By the time I left, the path was quiet again. But the mud bore witness: tiny parallel tracks leading from the woods to the creek, a temporary highway that would be gone by tomorrow. I made a note in my margin: "The forest has its own roads, and its own schedules. We are merely passing through."

Soundscape

What the forest sounds like, by season

Dawn · Spring
The Warbler Chorus

A cascade of thin, bright notes — wood warblers, blackthroated green, yellow-rumped. Each voice a different instrument, all playing at once. The sound rises from the canopy like water filling a glass.

Midday · Summer
Cicada Hum

A continuous, electric drone that seems to vibrate in your teeth. It's the sound of heat made audible. Beneath it, the occasional jay call, the whisper of wind through pine needles.

Dusk · Autumn
Woodpecker Drum

The pileated woodpecker's call — a raucous, laughing KA-KA-KA-KAH that echoes through thinning wood. Then the drumming: rapid, hollow, like someone tapping on a barrel. A sound that says: I am here, I am here, I am here.

Night · Winter
The Snow Silence

Not true silence — the wind still moves through bare branches, a dry rattling. Snow falls on snow with a sound like whispered secrets. An owl calls once, twice. The cold itself has a sound: a faint, high ringing at the edge of hearing.

Any time · Any season
Stream Over Stone

The constant. The baseline. Cedar Creek murmurs over its bed of smooth stones — a sound that changes with the season but never stops. In spring it roars. In summer it chuckles. In autumn it whispers. In winter it sometimes freezes into glass.

Afternoon · Late Summer
Fox in the Underbrush

The crackle of dry leaves, the snap of a small twig, then silence. A red fox moves through the forest floor with deliberate quiet. You hear it more than you see it — a rustling presence that stops whenever you stop.

About the Naturalist

I've been walking these woods for twelve years. Not every day — life, as it does, intervenes — but often enough that the seasons have become a second calendar, marked not by dates but by the first crocus, the last leaf, the moment when the ice cracks on the pond.

This notebook is the seventh volume. The earlier ones are dog-eared and coffee-stained, filled with sketches that improve slowly, observations that grow more precise, and a growing awareness of how little I truly know.

I am not a scientist, not in the formal sense. I have no degree in ecology, no research station, no grant funding. What I have is curiosity, a pair of good boots, and the willingness to sit quietly long enough for the forest to reveal itself.

Each entry here is true to the best of my knowledge. Some identifications may be wrong. Some dates may be approximate. But every observation was made with care, and every sketch was drawn by hand, in the field, with the smell of pine and damp earth still on my clothes.

The forest doesn't need me to document it. It will continue its slow, magnificent work whether I'm watching or not. But there's something about the act of paying attention — of really looking — that changes both the watcher and the watched. Or at least, it changes me.

— E. Whitfield

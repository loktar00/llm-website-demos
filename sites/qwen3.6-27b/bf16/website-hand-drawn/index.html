The Moss Notebook

a naturalist's field journal — one year, one woodland

Over twelve months of walking the same ridge trail, I filled this notebook with everything the forest offered: the first morel poking through last year's leaves, the redwing's dawn chorus from the alder thicket, the way light falls through beech canopy in October. These pages are an invitation to look closely.

Field Entries

— four turns of the wheel —

March — May

Spring

The beech floor trembles with bluebells by mid-April. On March 22, I found the year's first wood anemone beside the fallen ash—its white petals cupped tight against the chill, opening only when the sun crossed the ridge. Morels appeared in late April beneath the old cherry hedge: two at first, then a dozen. I sketched them in the field, knees deep in leaf litter, pencil smudged with damp soil.

↑ the smell of wet earth and decay — petrichore and possibility

June — August

Summer

Late June brought a procession of golden-ring dragonflies along the stream—males patrolling territories, females skimming the water to lay eggs in the submerged stems. The hazel nuts were tiny fists by July, and the air grew thick with the hum of bees in the clover meadow. I spent one hot afternoon simply watching a spider rebuild her orb web after the morning dew had weighed it down.

→ note: the green woodpecker returns every midday to the same sycamore stump

September — November

Autumn

October turned the sweet chestnuts to fire. I collected cones along the lower path—burs split open like porcelain grenades, nuts polished by woodpeckers. The first kingfisher darted across the stream on the 14th, a blue flash that stopped me mid-stride. Fungi erupted overnight: waxcaps on the grazed slope, parasols in the oak wood, a carpet of tiny fairy rings I had to kneel to photograph (and then immediately regret, reaching for my pencil instead).

↑ fairy ring of Mycena — perfect circle, 4.2m diameter

December — February

Winter

The forest reveals its bones in winter. Without leaves, the skeletal architecture of the beech stands stark against the grey sky. I walked the frost-rimed path on January 8 and found a tawny owl pellet beneath the holly—dissolved it in water and sorted out five bat wings, three vole skulls, a pile of cricket legs. The streams ran clear and cold, water veins silver against the dark stone. In the quiet, every twig-snap was a language.

→ silence here is not empty — it is full of listening

Specimen Notes

— seven things found, pressed, and pressed into memory —

No. 001

Silver Birch Leaf

Betula pendula — collected 14 June, pressed between pages of a borrowed copy of Thoreau. Note the doubly serrated margin, the asymmetrical base. Smells faintly of caramel when crushed.

No. 002

Golden Chanterelle

Cantharellus cibarius — found 3 September in the beech humus, half-buried in brown leaves. Decoyed me with its apricot scent. Sketched before photographing. The gills are actually blunt ridges, not true gills.

No. 003

Jay Primary Feather

Garrulus glandarius — found 11 April on the path near the old gate. Bright blue with white tip and buff base. The barbs had already begun to separate. I kept it for a week, then returned it to the trees.

No. 004

Seven-spot Ladybird

Coccinella septempunctata — observed 22 July on a blackthorn leaf. Seven spots, as the name declares, arranged with imperfect symmetry. The larvae look nothing like adults: elongate, spiny, almost alien. Nature loves a disguise.

No. 005

Scots Pine Cone

Pinus sylvestris — gathered 18 November after the frost had loosened them from the branches. Count the spirals: eight clockwise, thirteen anticlockockwise. Fibonacci again. The scales open in dry weather to release seeds, close in damp. A tiny mechanical weather station.

No. 006

Hart's Tongue Fern

Asplenium scolopendrium — growing on the north face of the sandstone wall, where the moss is deepest. Entire, undivided fronds — no pinnate splitting. The sori form two neat rows beneath the recurved margin. Unbroken, like a green ribbon.

No. 007

Owl Pellet Contents

Tyto alba / Myotis spp. — dissected 8 January beneath the holly tree. Five bat wings, three vole skulls, cricket legs, a fragment of mouse rib. The tawny owl eats what she can catch and regurgitates the rest — leaving us, the scavengers of scavengers, a miniature museum.

Featured Observation

— the one that stays with me —

The Kingfisher at Dawn — 14 October

It was just past 6:40, the light still blue and uncertain, when I heard it: a chattering rattle, like pebbles tumbling in a tin cup. I froze. The sound came from the stretch of stream below the old footbridge, where the water narrows between two stones and runs clear enough to see the gravel bed.

She appeared as a blue-grey blur against the dark water, perched on a dead hazel branch that juts from the far bank. Her head cocked, scanning. The beak — that impossible, spear-straight beak — pointed down at the current. I couldn't breathe. I hadn't even thought to reach for the pencil.

Then she was gone—a flash of electric blue, a silver ripple, and silence. Two seconds from perch to splash. I stood there for twenty minutes more, watching that same branch. She returned once, twice, each time disappearing with the same impossible speed. On the third dive, she came up with something small and silver flickering at the end of her beak. A minnow, maybe. Or a large stonefly nymph.

When I finally sat down to write, my hands were shaking. Not from cold—from the sheer intensity of paying attention, of being chosen as witness to a moment that didn't know it was being witnessed. I sketched the scene above, knowing the drawing would never capture the flash, only the memory of it.

"The kingfisher does not ask what color she is. She simply is blue, and the world makes room for it."

Soundscape

— what the forest sounds like, month by month —

Spring

Chiffchaff's cheer-up-little-leaf from the hazel. Cuckoo in the distance, a metronome set to longing. The drip-drip-drip of thaw from beech leaves. Woodpecker drumming — three rapid beats, pause, repeat — like a telegraph key.

Summer

The great hum: bees in clover, flies at ear-level, the low drone of the meadow. Nightingale at midnight — a cascading trill that builds and breaks like waves. Rustle of mice in the dead bracken. The stream's steady chatter.

Autumn

Redwing's soft reer-reer from the alder thickets. The crunch of frost-hardened leaves under boots. Pine cones dropping — a hollow thud. The first harsh call of the fieldfare, urgent and migrating. Wind in the beech, a long susurrus.

Winter

Silence, mostly. Then the sudden too-too of a woodcock from the wet hollow. The crack of freezing water in the stream. A single rook calling from the oak tops. Your own breathing, loud in the stillness. The scratch of a nuthark on bark.

About the Naturalist

I am not a professional naturalist. I am a person who, four years ago, decided to walk the same four-mile ridge trail every week regardless of weather, and to write down everything I noticed. This notebook is the result of one full year of that practice — from the first crocus through the final frost.

I work by day as a landscape architect, which means I draw land for a living. But here, in the moss-damp margins of Hawkridge Wood, I draw simply to see. The pencil slows the eye. The watercolor forces patience. The act of recording transforms a walk into a conversation.

My teacher is the forest itself, and it is a generous one. It has shown me that attention is a form of love, and that every small thing — a morel, a feather, the angle of light on a fern frond — deserves its own sentence.

"We do not inherit the earth from our ancestors; we borrow it from our children."

01 / Introduction

The collection

We hold 2,140 objects that answer one question: how does a city tell you where to stand? Maps, tokens, platform clocks, tactile strips, fare tables, departure boards — the silent grammar of public transit, gathered from fifty years of practice.

The Helvetica Transit Archive was founded in 1978 in the former signal works of a decommissioned station building. Its founding principle is that transit information design is typography before it is infrastructure: it is legible at 80 km/h, or it does not exist.

Nothing in the collection is hotlinked, scanned from official sources, or reproduced without permission. Every facsimile in this digital reading room was redrawn in-house, line by line, on an 8-millimetre grid.

04 / Timeline

Information design, 1872–2016

  1. 1872

    The first schematic map

    London prints a hand-drawn circular diagram of its underground lines — geography abandoned for legibility, eleven years before anyone called it “map design”.

    N° 01
  2. 1933

    The 45-degree turn

    Diagrammatic mapping is formalised: every route bends at 0° or 45°, every station becomes a dash on a line. Transit information becomes a typographic act.

    N° 02
  3. 1955

    Wayfinding as system

    Post-war expansion turns single maps into complete wayfinding systems: level diagrams, colour-coded zones, and the first consistent sign families across entire networks.

    N° 03
  4. 1968

    The modular sign

    An 8-millimetre module governs the platform: clock, timetable, advertisement and warning sign become variants of one grid. The station becomes a page.

    N° 04
  5. 1984

    Tactile and universal

    Guidance strips, raised lettering and pictographic standards extend the visual grammar to bodies in motion. Information design becomes accessibility law.

    N° 05
  6. 2016

    Living information

    Dynamic displays replace flip-strips. The grid survives; the ink becomes light. Our collection closes its static chapter and begins conserving software.

    N° 06
05 / Featured Essay

On the grammar of the sign

“A platform clock is not a decoration. It is the only promise the architecture makes to every single body on it — and it is kept to the second, or it is a lie in glass.”

When we acquire a ticket, we are not acquiring paper. We are acquiring a sentence with a verb: you may move, for this long, through these zones. The perforation is punctuation. The fare table is a footnote. The collection reads.

What unites the archive is not the object but the constraint — legibility at speed, in rain, at a glance, in a language the reader may not speak. These are the conditions under which the Swiss grotesque was born, and they remain the conditions under which it is still the hardest typeface in the world to do justice to.

Margrit Keller
Chief Curator · Published in the Annual, Vol. XLVII

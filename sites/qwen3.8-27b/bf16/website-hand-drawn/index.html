The Moss
Notebook

one year of forests, fungi, streams, birds & small wonders — recorded before they moved on

This is a working notebook, not a book. It has a creased spine, a corner of ink where the pen slipped in March, and a pressed bracken leaf that will outlive me. What follows is a year in one wood, drawn slowly and argued with. The drawings are honest; the memories are a little less so. Nothing here was taken. Everything here was noticed.

Four Turns of the Year

pinned in the order they happened

December — February

Winter · The Quiet Ledger

Blackthorn in flower while the ground is still iron. The run is a thread of dark water; I counted every crow and found the wood is mostly silence with a few opinions in it.

  • 12 Jan — first hoarfrost on the stile
  • 19 Jan — a woodpecker, three seconds, a whole morning's worth
  • 03 Feb — the wren sang before the light; brief as a coin
coldest: −9°, the 14th

March — May

Spring · The Wood Wakes Up Late

March came in like rain and left like mud. The hazel dropped catkins before the leaves dared; the hawthorn took its time and paid for it in eggs. By May the ferns had uncurled enough to hide a person.

  • 14 Mar — the snail study (see field study, p. 14)
  • 02 Apr — first eggs: three, in a hawthorn huddle
  • 21 May — ferns at waist height; the whole ground breathing
rainiest: March, again

June — August

Summer · Long Light, Small Water

The run is barely a thread now, and the ants have a highway where it used to be. The light runs past nine and the notebook gets kept open until my shadow is a blue smear. Everything grows so loudly you stop hearing the wren.

  • 08 Jun — a kingfisher, one blue argument
  • 29 Jul — longest day; drew until the ferns closed
  • 17 Aug — heat so still the bees flew in a line
no rain, 26 days (July)

September — November

Autumn · The Fungus Shift

September kept the sun; October took the trees. The ground underfoot went soft and honest — brackets glowing after rain, toadstools in processions, the oak laying out its galls like a tax. The wood smells of wet wool and something sweet.

  • 05 Sep — oak galls, green coins
  • 12 Oct — a bracket the size of a hat; "the cathedral"
  • 02 Nov — acorn tithe; the jays were in a hurry
best fungi day: 12 Oct

Field Study · The Hour of the Snail

14 March · 07:40–09:12 · drizzle, then nothing

I came to the run that morning to count the wrens, because the wrens are the wood's metronome and I have been counting them for eleven years. I met a snail instead — or rather, a snail met me on the second step of the bank, and the snail, I will not insult you by pretending, took over the morning.

It was a grove snail, one of the striped kind, its shell a pale keel with seams like a river chart, and it was working the wet ground with the seriousness of an archaeologist. Its trail of slime caught the drizzle and glistened, which is to say: the snail was carrying a small river behind it, and neither of us said anything about it.

I sat down. This is the part I have to be honest about. I came with a pencil, a pocket of crumbs, and a plan for one hour; I left with a pencil, an empty pocket, and no plan. The snail had moved four inches. I have never watched anything move four inches so convincingly.

Patience, I am beginning to suspect, is just another word for paying attention longer than the moment lasts.

There is a taxonomy of wet I am assembling, and the snail is its first serious subject: drizzle, which the wood drinks; mist, which the wood wears; dew, which the wood keeps on the fern tips like a promise; and then this — ground-water wet, the wet that lives below the soil and only surfaces in snail-trail and mushroom-cap and the shine of the bank at 08:12. All of them the same water, I am told. I believe the water. I am not sure the categories survive the morning.

I counted fourteen snails on the bank in the end, no two of them alike, each one a small argument against my schedule. The wrens, to their credit, kept the count going whether I listened or not. I left before the snail finished what it was doing. That is the rule of the notebook: you take nothing, and you are left, slowly, with everything.

What the Wood Says

written down, since I can't press play on a forest
06:40 · April

The wren, at dawn

One small engine of a song, impossibly loud for its chest; the wood goes still in rows, like listeners.

anytime · the run

Water over the stones

A sentence the run repeats to itself, changing one word each time. I have not once understood it; I have never wanted to.

15:20 · October

Wind in the beech

The whole canopy leaning, and then letting go — a long grey sigh with leaves in it.

18:05 · November

Crows, at dusk

A parliament of static; the sky briefly rearranged, then filed under again.

03:10 · March

Rain on the brake

Ten thousand small taps, all different sizes — a drum that tunes itself and never asks if you're listening.

midnight · July

The dark

The crickets start their metronome; a bat's applause; then the owl — one low line — and silence with edges.

The Hand Behind the Pencil

self-portrait, third attempt, most honest
M. Aldercott

M. Aldercott has been keeping this notebook for eleven years, from the same stile, in the same wood, with the same pencil broken in the same place. I am not a scientist — or I was once, before once was allowed. I am a person who counts. What I count is not the important thing. What I draw is the important thing. The wood corrects me; I have learned to take the corrections in pencil.

By day I repair things for a living; by dusk I am usually somewhere between the stile and the run, drawing whatever has been holding its ground. The notebook is the only house I have ever kept tidy.

  1. Count slowly.
  2. Leave the biggest one.
  3. Weather is a collaborator, not an excuse.
  4. If it is moving, it is alive, or it is the wind. Check.

( 01 )Introduction to the collection

A Museum of Telling

A timetable is a promise. The Archive collects the objects that keep it — the map that promises space, the clock that promises time, the sign that promises you will not be lost.

Scope of the collection

Founded in 1987 around a single Zürich platform clock, the Archive now holds over twelve thousand objects from sixty-one transit authorities: printed network diagrams, enamel and vinyl signage, punched and magnetic tickets, platform timepieces, split-flap boards, pictogram masters and the drafting sheets beneath them. The criterion is narrow and strict — the object must have worked, in public, under time pressure.

We exhibit working systems, not nostalgia. Every artefact is shown at full scale beside the specification that produced it, on a grid built to the same discipline the objects obey. What the railway did for space and time, this museum attempts for attention: nothing decorative survives the platform.

( 04 )1841 — 2019 · nine entries

Chronology of Information Design

1841

The Cardboard Ticket

Ticketing

Railway clerk Thomas Edmondson patents a numbered card ticket, printed in batches and validated by date. The format outlives nearly every company that adopted it.

1933

The Octolinear Diagram

Mapping

Harry Beck submits a hand-drawn Underground diagram that trades geography for geometry. Seven hundred thousand copies in the first year; the map has been redrawn, never redesigned, since.

1944

The Station Clock

Time

Hans Hilfiker’s Bahnhofsuhr enters service: a white field, black batons, and a red second hand that completes its sweep in 58.5 seconds and waits for the minute.

1965

One Railway, One Symbol

Identity

The Design Research Unit reduces a national railway to two arrows. Ticket, sign, livery and timetable obey a single grid; the passenger stops noticing the design, which is the point.

1968

The Pictographic Capital

Symbols

Lance Wyman’s Olympic programme proves a whole city can be written in symbols: stations, venues and streets share one geometric vocabulary, legible at a run.

1972

Systems, Not Posters

Systems

Otl Aicher’s pictograms guide Munich’s new U-Bahn while Vignelli’s diagram cuts Manhattan loose from its geography. Information design becomes infrastructure, in two hemispheres at once.

1990

The Network as Timetable

Mapping

Zürich’s S-Bahn opens with a diagram drawn from the interval, not the street plan: distances are paced in minutes, and the map reads like a schedule you can walk through.

2003

The Journey Becomes Data

Ticketing

Contactless ticketing reaches London’s gates. The paper ticket begins its long retirement; the journey is now recorded before the train has arrived.

2019

The Map Queries Itself

Software

Open journey planners render networks from live feeds. For the first time since 1933, the diagram is drawn anew for every passenger, on every query.

Letters at the Speed of Walking

Nobody reads an airport. They scan it — at speed, wheeling a suitcase, chased by a departure time. When the new Paris terminals commissioned Adrian Frutiger in the early 1970s, he was not asked to make letters beautiful. He was asked to make them survive.

Frutiger had already mapped an entire family onto a numbered grid; the airport brief inverted that rigour. Instead of a system for printers, he built one for strangers in motion: apertures that stay open under vibration and bad light, generous counters, a tall x-height that reads like a raised voice without ever shouting. The typeface does not greet you. It instructs you, and steps aside.

“A wayfinding face is not read — it is obeyed. The traveller should absorb the instruction and never notice the letters that delivered it.” — Archive transcript, wayfinding seminar, Paris, 1974 (tape 12)

Helvetica arrived on the platform by a different road. Its apertures close, its rhythm is even, its temperament is deliberately indifferent — the ideal courier for a timetable, which must not editorialise. Where Frutiger’s letters lean toward the hurrying passenger, Helvetica stands square and lets the passenger come to it. Zürich understood this. On its platform signs and its diagram, the even voice reads as confidence: the train will come; the letters do not have to insist.

Fig. 04 — Specimen row, set in the Archive’s substitute grotesque. The red is ours.

The Archive keeps both temperaments in adjacent vitrines, along with a drawer of rejected trials where the two philosophies argue across a drafting table: a lowercase “a” widened until it could be read from a moving escalator; a “G” whose spur was removed because, at sixty metres, a spur is a rumour.

Wayfinding typefaces are the only literature every citizen reads. No one chooses them, everyone uses them, almost no one remembers them. That forgetting is the achievement. A sign succeeds when it disappears into the act of going — and a city becomes legible at the speed of walking.

The Archive is an editorial fiction; the airports, the railways and the typefaces are not. Specifications are condensed, arguments abridged, and the red is always Nº 3020.

Part One · Four Seasons, Four Visits

Field Entries

written on the day, honest about the weather

I go back to the same wood once a season, sit on the same stone, and write what changes. Mostly it is the light. Sometimes it is everything.

pl. 1 — fiddlehead, first week of unfolding

Spring17 March · first light · 4°C

The stream is loud again

The fiddleheads have unrolled along the ditch behind the stone bridge — eleven of them, counted twice, each curled like a slow question. Mire Brook is running the colour of strong tea, and the chiffchaff that arrived overnight has already said its own name four hundred times by breakfast.

frogspawn: 9 clumps in the shallow pool — up from 5 last year

I sat by the pool until my knees complained. Under the old beech the wood anemones are open in ones and twos, and every bee that finds them sounds personally delighted. Nothing in this wood is in a hurry except the water.

pl. 2 — the pond's smallest landlord

Summer22 June · mid-afternoon · 26°C in the shade

The stream goes quiet

Mire Brook has shrunk to a whisper; I crossed at the stepping stones without wetting a boot. A southern hawker has claimed the pond, patrolling its border like a very small, very fast park-keeper. Every frog in the county has gone sober and silent.

grass is bee-high on the south bank — lost my pencil in it, found a beetle instead

I sketched the same thistle for an hour. A goldfinch tore it apart afterwards with more confidence than my drawing deserved. In the evening the bats came out over the water, five of them, stitching the air where the midges were thinking about being.

pl. 3 — the leaf that landed on the page

Autumn9 October · before dawn · mist

Loud with losing everything

The boletes arrived overnight beneath the beech — six caps the colour of burnt toffee, one with a slug already in residence. Redwings ticked overhead in the dark before first light, the first of the winter, punctual as rent.

left the spore print on the kitchen table; the cat approved

The wood is at its loudest while it loses everything. Leaves come down like slow confetti, and every one of them lands with a small opinion about where it should be allowed to rest.

pl. 4 — the twig outside the bothy window

Winter30 December · hard frost · ink at 4°C and objecting

Loud with absence

Frost has drawn its own ferns on the bothy window, better than I could. The wood is loud with absence — every crow sounds like an argument, every footstep like a confession. The pond wears a lid of ice with one corner kept open, stubbornly, by the spring.

feeling in toes: none. tea consumed: 4 mugs. regret: none.

I left half an apple on the flat stone by the bridge, for whoever is awake at this hour. By the time I came back down the hill it was gone, and the snow around it was politely unsignposted.

Part Four · One Hour, Slowly

The Grey Hour

14 October · 5:40 → 6:55 a.m. · the stone bridge
wind changed at six — everything moved one reed to the left
N.B. — the thermos leaks. see repairs, p. 40

I arrived in the dark on purpose, which is a thing you can only do to yourself. The bothy kettle had just boiled; I walked down with the thermos warming my ribs and the path known so well that my boots did the thinking. At the bridge the water was a sound before it was a sight — a long grey consonant, the wood clearing its throat.

Before first light a wood is all ear. A wren scolded me from the bramble, personally, at length. Somewhere behind the beech a wood pigeon rearranged itself twice and settled. The redwings had gone quiet after their night flight; the mist lay in the meadow like spilled milk, and the river made the only weather there was.

Then the heron came — the heron, the one that has fished this bend for three years and never once paid for the privilege. It did not arrive so much as get decided: a grey suggestion above the shallows that resolved, wing-beat by patient wing-beat, into a bird. It folded itself down onto the stone shallows and became furniture. I have been looked at by that heron for three years. It has never once looked back.

"Patience is just attention, kept warm."

The grey hour is the honest one. Colour arrives later, like a late guest with an excuse. For a long while the water was pewter, the reeds were charcoal, the heron was a rumour of itself. I made three studies and threw two away; the third kept the shape of the waiting if not the bird. A kingfisher went through the picture I was not drawing — a struck match, then out again — and the whole wood pretended not to have seen it.

At 6:20 the light arrived the colour of weak tea. The heron struck once, missed with theatrical dignity, and then, because nobody was watching, caught a minnow the length of my thumb. It took the bird ninety seconds to turn the fish, and me four minutes of held breath. It swallowed, resumed being furniture, and I wrote the only sentence of the morning that has survived editing: the river keeps its own ledger; we only borrow the light to read it.

It left at ten to seven, the way a letter leaves a hand — with two slow wingbeats, then the morning closed over the place where it had been. I poured the last of the thermos, which had spent the hour quietly leaking into my satchel, and said thank you to nobody in particular. The wren, still on duty, told me exactly what it thought of that.

Part Six · The Ear Pages

A Small Anthology of Noise

press a circle — a rough memory, whistled by the machine

Mire Brook, at the stepping stones

A continuous conversation between water and stone. In spate it argues with itself; in August it keeps its voice down and gossips about the weather.

all year

Woodpecker, on the dead beech

Roughly eighteen beats in under two seconds, delivered to a resonant trunk like a drummer testing a hall. He pauses afterwards to be admired. He is usually rewarded.

Feb → Apr

Tawny owl, asking its one question

Two notes from the ivy on the old beech, the second always longer, as if the first needed justifying. I have answered it out loud twice. No reply. Neutral result.

winter dusks

Wind in the seven pines

The top boughs say a long hhhh and the valley keeps the consonant. It arrives from the west in paragraphs, with pauses you could hang a coat on.

whenever it likes

these are memories of the real thing — the originals are played live, most evenings, free.

The Last Page Before the Pocket

About the Naturalist

Marlow Finch is a field naturalist of no formal distinction and one durable habit: going back. For thirty-one years, in forty-one notebooks, the same wood has been walked, sat in, drawn badly, drawn slightly better, and written about in ink that ran when it rained — which, in this county, is often.

The method is simple and cannot be improved: arrive early, sit still, write down what happened, then draw what you remember — because the drawing keeps what the photograph would only rent. Nothing here is killed, framed, or pressed without apology. The wood is consulted. The wood has never objected to being sketched, only to being hurried.

The Moss Notebook is volume forty-one, kept from one March to the next. The previous forty are in a biscuit trunk under the bothy bed, in loose order, in sepia, waiting for a wet week and a good lamp.

The Sea Machine

Thirty-one thousand square metres of board-marked concrete sunk into a basalt headland so that a coast can drink. The Point Nero works is the largest object in this archive — and the only one that manufactures a river where there is none.

You reach it the way its architect intended: by the coast road, at the speed the coast road allows. From the bend above the olive terraces the plant does not so much appear as accumulate — three stepped halls of basalt-aggregate concrete, descending toward the water like something set down, reconsidered, and set down again closer. The intake sits fourteen metres below the surface, two hundred and fourteen metres offshore, behind a capping structure the fishermen use as a landmark and, unofficially, as a mooring. The building begins long before the building begins.

Inside, the membrane halls are the argument. Two sheds, forty metres each, more than six thousand pressure vessels racked in ranks so even they read as an instrument. Mara Vela was thirty-four when she won the commission, and she spent her first year refusing the client's plan to bury the halls in an earth berm. A pump is a pump, she told the review board in 1991, until you give it a nave. Then she gave it one: a six-metre clerestory running the full length of both halls, so the machinery is lit like something worth looking at. It is worth looking at. We checked, twice, on principle.

"A pump is a pump
until you give it a nave."
— MARA VELA, REVIEW BOARD TRANSCRIPT, 1991

The plant is not uncontroversial, and the archive does not pretend otherwise. The brine outfall — a four-hundred-millimetre diffuser line running to the forty-metre contour — was litigated for four years and is monitored to this day. What ended the argument, as much as anything ends such arguments, was a civic gesture: Vela's office installed a public letterboard at the site gate on which the daily discharge figures are chalked by hand, every morning, at six. As far as we can establish, it remains the only desalination plant in Europe with a letterboard.

The third gesture is the one we came for. In 1989 the commune forced an eleven-metre right-of-way through the middle of the site — the old coastal footpath, never extinguished. Vela did not fight it. She paved it in board-marked concrete, roofed nothing, signed nothing, and let it run level with the works behind a plain concrete balustrade. You walk the plant's whole length, close enough to touch the intake pump house. On the second day of our survey a fisherman mended nets in the shade of Hall B's buttress, inside the fence, unhurried, unchallenged, and entirely entitled to be there. That, more than any elevation, is what the building argues.

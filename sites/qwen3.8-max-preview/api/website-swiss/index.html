The Collection

Sammlung / Introduction

The Helvetica Transit Archive preserves the visual infrastructure of public mobility: network maps, platform signage, printed tickets, station clocks and dynamic information systems. Each object is treated as a primary document of information design — evidence of how cities taught millions of strangers to move with confidence, using nothing more than a grid, a typeface and one disciplined accent of red.

Acquisition policy. Objects enter the collection only in working condition or documented unrestored state. Reproductions are marked and never exhibited beside originals.

Reading room. The map and ticket cabinets are open to researchers by appointment, Tuesday to Friday.

Netzplan Study — «Stadt am Fluss»

SVG · 45° Geometry
Linie A — Südring ⇄ Flughafen Linie B — Nordwies ⇄ Brückfeld Linie C — Westpark ⇄ Alpenblick

Study notes. The diagram reconstructs a fictional mid-sized network using the canonical rules of the 1960s Swiss school: octilinear routing, uniform station intervals, interchange markers doubled in weight, and a single warm accent reserved for the primary diameter line.

The river is drawn as negative information — a pale band that orients without competing. Labels are set flush to the line grid, never rotated.

Timeline of Information Design

1944 — 2004
1944
The Station Clock Standardised
A single dial with bar indices and a red sweeping second hand is adopted across the federal network, synchronised by impulse telegraphy. Time becomes a public interface.
Clock
1957
Grotesk Becomes the Voice
Neutral sans-serif faces displace serifed railway lettering. Signage copy is fixed at upper- and lower-case, with letter-spacing tuned for 60-metre reading distances.
Typography
1962
The Diagrammatic Map Wins
Geographic accuracy is formally abandoned in favour of topology: straightened lines, equalised spacing, and the interchange symbol as the map's only punctuation.
Map
1968
Integrated Wayfinding Systems
Signage, maps and printed matter are designed as one grammar — a fixed grid, one typeface family, one accent colour — deployed across entire stations.
System
1972
Pictogram Grids
Figurative symbols are redrawn on square construction grids so they survive reproduction from enamel to a 9 mm ticket imprint without losing legibility.
Pictogram
1983
The Machine Joins the System
Ticket machines and early dot-matrix departure displays are brought under the same design ordinance; the red key becomes the universal verb for «confirm».
Interface
1994
Night Networks & Zoning
24-hour operation forces new map editions. Fare zones are drawn as concentric rings; the map and the ticket finally share one geometry.
Map
2004
Analogue Grammar, Digital Screens
Dynamic LED and LCD displays inherit the bar-index clock, the 45° diagram and the red accent — proof that a rigorous system survives every change of medium.

Featured Designer

Essay / Schrift
Margrit Odermatt-Brunner · 1934–2016

«She did not design signs. She designed the certainty of arriving.»

Margrit Odermatt-Brunner trained as a graphic artist in Lucerne before joining the federal railways' design office in 1961, one of the first women admitted to its drafting room. Within a decade she had rewritten the signage ordinance for forty-two stations, insisting that every decision be defensible by measurement: reading distance, walking speed, dwell time at the platform edge.

Her method was arithmetic before artistry. She fixed the letter height of platform signs to a formula of distance and velocity, then derived every other size from it by simple ratios. The result was a family of signs that felt inevitable — as if they had always been there, which was precisely her ambition.

Odermatt-Brunner reserved red with surgical restraint. One red element per sightline, never two: the red was a verb, an instruction, a point of decision. Everything else belonged to black and white, the colours of the printed grid she loved.

Her 1974 manual, Richtung / Direction, remains the archive's most requested volume. It opens with a single sentence that still hangs above our reading room door: «First the passenger, then the plan, and only then the drawing.»

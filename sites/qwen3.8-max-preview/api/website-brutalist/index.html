Coastal water infrastructure
FEATURE — CAPE MURO DESALINATION & PUMPING STATION

The sea, metered.

Tideworks No.7 does not greet you. It stands at the end of a basalt breakwater like a machine that has decided something on behalf of the whole coast: that salt will be removed, that pressure will be held, that the city forty kilometres inland will open its taps without ever knowing this place exists. The building is 310 metres long, windowless for its first two hundred, and poured in a single continuous operation over nineteen days in the winter of 1976.

“Ornament was budgeted. It was then spent on wall thickness.”

The engineers — the firm of Brandt & Øye, working under a municipal directive that survives only as a stamped carbon copy — specified marine-grade concrete at 600 kilograms per cubic metre of cement, then added ten percent and stopped apologising. Wave return on this stretch of coast is measured in decades, not seasons. The structure was designed to be hit, scoured, and ignored.

Inside, the hall reads like a section drawing come loose: intake flumes at −9.000, membrane racks at +4.500, and above them the crane rail, unused for eleven years, kept because removal would have cost more than patience. Every surface carries its formwork. Nothing has been painted. The archive acquired the original setting-out drawings in 2019; they smell faintly of brine to this day.

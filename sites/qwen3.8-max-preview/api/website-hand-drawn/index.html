A Year in the Hollow

Selected pages from the notebook — smudges, rain-warping and all.

14 March · first true thaw

Spring — The Stream Wakes

The ice on Alder Run went honeycomb-soft overnight and by noon the stream was talking again — a low, gossipy murmur under the footbridge. I found the year's first frogspawn in a still pool: black beads in glass jelly, each one a small held breath. Along the bank the moss has turned that impossible spring green, luminous as if lit from inside. I sketched for an hour and got cold feet and warm hands and would not trade either.

note to self: frogspawn always on the shallow side of the bend →

21 June · solstice, 9pm light

Summer — Firefly Census

Sat in the meadow edge with a tally sheet and a jar of weak tea. First flash at 9:47, low over the bent grass; by 10:15 the whole lower field was blinking like a slow conversation in Morse. Counted forty-one distinct lanterns before I gave up counting and simply watched. The moss on the north stones stays damp even now — I pressed my thumb to it and it sprang back, holding the print for a full minute. Summer is loud above and quiet below.

the wrens fed young all evening — five mouths, one very tired parent

9 October · amber light, still air

Autumn — The Fungal Bloom

After three days of soft rain the forest floor simply erupted. I counted eleven species on my usual twenty-minute loop: chanterelles in apricot processions, stinkhorns smelling gloriously of their own bad ideas, and one enormous chicken-of-the-woods shelving out of an oak like a stack of orange books. The moss is doing its quiet autumn work too — spore capsules everywhere, tiny copper lanterns on hair-fine stalks. I came home with spores in my cuffs and no regrets.

do NOT eat the pretty one by the birch. (Write that twice.)

28 January · hoarfrost, −6°

Winter — Reading the Snow

The hollow wore a white page this morning and every creature had signed it. Deer tracks in neat pairs of exclamation marks heading for the cedar shelter; a fox's chain of stitches crossing the stream ice; and the tiny sewing-machine script of a wren along the wall. Under the frost the moss is dormant but not dead — I lifted a fringe of haircap and found green as patient as a held note. Winter is not absence. It is the forest writing in a smaller hand.

tracks end at the old gate — fox went over, not around. respect.

The Night the Moss Glowed

23 September · written by torchlight, corrected by daylight

I went out that night for one reason only: to hear the tawny owls. The forecast had promised still air and a three-quarter moon, and in September those two things together turn the hollow into an amphitheatre. I took the lantern low, the way you're supposed to, and let my eyes do the walking.

The owls obliged within ten minutes — the female's contact call first, a soft questioning ku-witt from the beeches, then the male's full quavering hoot rolling down off the knoll like something poured rather than sung. I sat on the fallen log and wrote nothing for a while because some things defeat a pencil on the first attempt.

"I had walked past this wall of light a hundred times in daylight and never once seen it."

It was on the walk home that the second, unplanned observation happened. My torch beam swept the bank beside the stream and came back of its own accord, because something in the beam had answered. A faint, greenish, impossible glow, low in the moss. I crouched. I switched the torch off. I switched it on again. There it was: foxfire, bioluminescent mycelium threading through the moss and the rotting alder stump, glowing with the patience of something that has never once needed to be seen.

I sat with it for forty minutes. The glow never pulsed or flickered; it simply was, steady as a held note, brighter whenever my dark-adapted eyes settled and dimmer whenever I leaned in too eagerly, as if it resented being inspected. A slug passed through it and became briefly, magnificently illuminated from below. I laughed out loud, alone, at a slug. This is what fieldwork does to a person and I recommend it.

Later, at the kitchen table, I looked it up with damp hair and muddy knees: Mycena chlorophos is the famous one, but our modest glow was more likely the mycelium of a honey fungus or a Panellus, feeding quietly in the wet wood. The textbooks gave me a mechanism — luciferin, oxygen, an enzyme — and the mechanism explained everything and also nothing at all.

What I keep from that night is not the taxonomy. It is this: I had walked past that bank a hundred times in daylight and never once seen it. The moss had been glowing there, presumably, on every still, damp night of the year, keeping its small green appointment with the dark, and I had simply never thought to go and look. The woods do not perform for us. We have to meet them halfway, at their hour, in their weather, with our eyes properly useless and finally ready.

— returned three nights running after. The glow was there each time. The owls, smugly, were too.

soundscapes · transcribed loosely

What the Hollow Sounds Like

You can't press a sound between pages. You can try.

dawn chorus · late may

The Wall of Song

It starts as single threads — one robin, then a blackbird stitching long silver phrases through the dark — and then, all at once, the whole hedge is woven. The wrens add a crackle of static, the chiffchaff its two patient syllables, and the air itself seems to thicken with it. By full light the sound is so dense you can lean on it like weather.

the stream · any month

Water Talking to Itself

Under the footbridge the run deepens and the voice drops to a round, woody murmur, like someone reading aloud in the next room. After rain it brightens to a chatter; in drought it thins to a whisper over the stones. Learn it long enough and you can tell the weather from the pitch alone.

october rain · under canopy

Ten Thousand Small Drums

Rain on beech leaves is soft and sibilant; rain on the broad oak leaves is a slower, heavier applause; rain on a dead log is all knuckles and wood. Stand still under the canopy and the whole forest becomes percussion, every surface playing its own part, and your hood the loudest drum of all.

january snowfall · midday

The Quietest Sound There Is

Snow swallows the high notes first, then the middle ones, until all that's left is your own breathing and the small felt-slip of flakes landing on your sleeve. Even the stream seems to lower its voice. It is not silence, exactly. It is the world holding still to listen, and you doing the same.

kit list · checked twice, lost once

What Goes in the Rucksack

Everything a naturalist needs, and several things she merely loves carrying.

  • This notebook — waxed cover, cream paper, survives light rain and one full dunking
  • 2B and 4B pencils, sharpened the night before (always forgotten by noon)
  • One waterproof ink pen, brown-black, for the outlines that must not run
  • Half a watercolour tin: moss green, ochre, a sky blue that's nearly finished
  • 10× hand lens on a lanyard — the single most important object I own
  • Collapsible paper bag for fallen things (feathers, husks, one memorable snail shell)
  • Thermos of weak tea, strong opinions about biscuit pairing
  • String, for measuring nothing in particular and tying everything up
  • Wool hat in four seasons; the woods do not care about dignity
  • Field guide to fungi — lost in the woods, September. If found, it's yours now.
about the naturalist

The Hand Holding the Pencil

Wren Halloran is a field naturalist and lifelong doodler who has kept a notebook for every year since she was nine, when she wrote a full, stern report on a single pond skater. This is volume seven of the moss years — the ones spent mostly looking down, which she maintains is where most of the world is.

She walks the same few square miles of Alder Hollow on purpose, believing that a small patch of ground, visited often enough, becomes infinite. The drawings in this notebook are done on site, in weather, at whatever speed the subject allows: snails are patient sittings; kingfishers are one startled line.

Nothing here was collected, harmed, or hurried. The moss was pressed and released. The feather was held and returned. The glow was watched, and then left to its own devices, which is the only respectful thing to do with a light that never asked for an audience.

— W.H., by the stove, boots still damp

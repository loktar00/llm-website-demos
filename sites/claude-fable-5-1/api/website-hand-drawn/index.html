Notebook

a naturalist's hand-drawn record of forests, fungi, streams, birds and the very small things underfoot

Kept between the first frost of one January and the next, in a valley I will not name precisely. Everything here was drawn on the spot or the same evening, from memory and a pocketful of specimens.

§ 01 · Field entries, one per season

Four days out of three hundred and sixty-five

The notebook has a hundred and some entries. These are the four I keep turning back to, one for each turn of the valley.

Entry 14

Spring · 21 March

Hazel coppice, after rain

Rain until nine, then a thin sun that made the whole coppice tick and drip. Wood anemones are open along the north ride, six petals apiece except for one stubborn seven. Hazel catkins have gone from stiff to loose in a fortnight; when I flicked one, a cloud of pollen hung for a full second in the still air.

The chiffchaff arrived — the first — calling from the same holly it used last year, which I find quietly astonishing.

seven petals — kept the drawing, not the flower

Entry 51

Summer · 14 July

Aldery Beck, below the ford

The beck is down to its bones. Where it ran knee-deep in April I can cross on stones without wetting a boot. Azure damselflies stitch back and forth over the pool; I counted eleven and gave up when they moved.

Under the alder roots a bullhead sat perfectly still while a shadow of water-crowfoot moved over it. Heat by midday. Wrote this sitting in the water.

water 14°, air 27°. feet: numb, happy

Entry 77

Autumn · 9 October

Beech hanger, under the big trees

The flush has begun. Overnight, or so it seems, the litter under the beeches is punctuated with fungi: amethyst deceivers in a scatter of violet, a troop of shaggy inkcaps already dissolving at the edges, and one immaculate fly agaric I sat beside for an hour, drawing.

Beech leaves are copper at the crown, still green below. A nuthatch worked the trunk above me, upside down and unbothered.

smell: wet pennies & pear

Entry 103

Winter · 18 January

Kestrel Bank, hard frost

Hard frost, −4° at dawn. Every grass blade on the bank carried its own white fur. Tracks in the skim of snow along the beck: fox, purposeful; a heron's careful scrawl at the margin; the tiny ladder of a wren under the bramble.

The wren itself appeared later, furious as always, and shouted at me from a distance of two feet. Ink froze in the pen. Finished in pencil.

pencil from here on — note the smudges

§ 03 · Seven specimen notes

Things small enough to carry home in an envelope

Or in the case of the fly agaric, to sit next to. Latin names are my best effort; corrections gratefully accepted at the third bench.

No. 01coppice edge · 3 Apr

Leucobryum glaucum Cushion moss, pin-cushion, "the pillow"

where
north edge of the coppice, on acid humus under holly
size
a cushion 14 cm across, 5 cm deep
colour
glaucous grey-green; near-white when dry

Dense enough to bounce a pencil off. Pulled a single stem free: leaves crowded, incurved, with a swollen midrib. It went ghost-white on the windowsill by Tuesday and green again the moment I breathed on it.

→ this is the moss the notebook is named for. Obviously.

No. 02beech hanger · 9 Oct

Amanita muscaria Fly agaric — the storybook one

where
litter under beech and birch, mid-slope of the hanger
cap
11 cm, scarlet fading to orange at the rim, warts rain-washed
smell
faint, earthy, unremarkable

Drawn, not picked. Gills free and white, ring skirt-like and pendulous, base bulbous with concentric scales. Three more buttons pushing up beside it like small red eggs. A slug had already eaten a perfectly semicircular bite from the rim and seemed fine.

NB: birch above, beech beside. Which is it partnering?

No. 03north ride · 2 Jun

Athyrium filix-femina Lady fern — one pinna, pressed

where
damp bank of the north ride, in deep shade
frond
c. 80 cm; this pinna 14 cm, from the middle third
habit
lacy, bipinnate, arching, absurdly delicate

Pinnules toothed, tapering; sori small and comma-shaped along the underside, still pale green in June. Stipe with dark brown scales at the base. The whole plant moves in air I cannot feel, which is how I always find it.

pressed between pp. 40–41. It stained the page green. Good.

No. 04top of hanger · 22 Aug

Garrulus glandarius Jay — one blue wing covert

where
path along the top of the hanger, after a screaming match between two of them
length
4.6 cm; barred blue, black and white
note

The bars are not quite regular; each one wobbles like a hand-drawn line, which pleased me more than it should. Thirteen dark bars on this one. The base is downy and warm grey. Kept in the envelope marked "impossible blue".

jays planted half this wood. Acorn by acorn. Forgetful gardeners.

No. 05the fallen beech · 19 May

Carabus nemoralis Bronze ground beetle

where
under loose bark on the fallen beech, alarmed
length
23 mm; bronze with a green-violet margin, three rows of dimples per elytron
behaviour
ran, stopped, ran, bit my thumb

Held for forty seconds, drawn from memory in about forty minutes. The dimples catch light like hammered metal. Released where found; it went straight back under the same flap of bark, which I read as a review of my hospitality.

flightless. Nocturnal. Eats slugs — an ally.

No. 06Otter Pool edge · 30 Jun

Cepaea nemoralis Brown-lipped banded snail

where
on nettles at the pool edge, mid-morning, mid-dew
shell
21 mm; yellow ground with three chocolate bands, lip dark brown
company
four others within arm's reach, no two banded alike

The variation is the whole point of this species and I could spend a month on one nettle patch. This one: bands 1, 2 and 3 fused, 4 and 5 absent, in the collector's notation I learned that afternoon and have already half forgotten. Thrushes leave the empty shells on a flat stone by the ford — their anvil.

No. 07old beech · 14 Dec

Lecanora chlarotera Rim lichen, on beech bark — with ID caveat

where
smooth bark, north face of the oldest standing beech
form
grey-green crust, 6 cm across, with warty rim to each fruiting disc
certainty
moderate. Lichens humble me monthly.

Drawn with the lens balanced on a twig. The discs — apothecia — are tan with a pale margin the same colour as the thallus, which is the rimmed part of "rim lichen". Growing at a millimetre a year, this patch is older than I am. Bark flake kept; lichen still alive on it, I think.

ask someone who knows. Then draw it again.

§ 04 · A long observation

One Log, Twelve Months

The beech that came down in the February gale, and everything that moved in after it.

It fell on the night of the twelfth of February, a mature beech perhaps a hundred and forty years old, and when I found it the next morning the wound in the hanger was still raw with the smell of split wood. It had taken a young holly down with it and lay across the ride with its root plate lifted like a door. I decided, standing there, that I would visit it every month for a year and write down whatever I saw. This is that record, compressed.

March: nothing, or nothing I was equipped to notice. The bark was tight and grey and smelt of nothing. A treecreeper used the upturned roots as a perch, which was the first use anything made of the tree in its new life. April brought the first bark beetles — tiny, bronze, purposeful — and a pattern of frass (see margin) like fine sawdust in the crevices near the crown.

By June the bark on the underside had begun to lift. Slugs and a great many woodlice had moved into the gap. I found the first fungal mycelium here as white fans under a loosened sheet of bark, cool to the touch and smelling faintly of mushroom soup. A wren was nesting somewhere in the root plate; I never found the nest, which is as it should be.

September was the turning point. Oyster mushrooms — a ledge of them, grey and overlapping like roof tiles — appeared on the north side. Two weeks later came honey fungus in clumps at the base, and from then on the log was never without something fruiting. I drew the oysters three times over ten days as they went from buttons to plates to a soft brown collapse, and each drawing is worse than the one before because I liked them more.

Winter slowed everything but stopped nothing. Turkey tail brackets in December, velvet-fringed and banded in the colours of wet slate. A wood mouse's cache of beech mast tucked into a knot hole, seventeen nuts, counted and replaced. And moss — the moss of this notebook's name — appearing in a green line exactly along the shaded top of the trunk, as if someone had drawn it with a ruler.

The gale that killed the tree was the beginning of a great deal. I had thought of a fallen tree as an ending and it is closer to an opening: a door, as the root plate had suggested on that first morning. I will keep visiting. The log has perhaps thirty years of stories in it and I would like to hear a few more of them before it is soil.

Feb 12, 02:40. Gusts of 62 mph at the station in town, the radio said. Here it sounded like the sea.
frass — the polite word for insect droppings mixed with sawdust. Fine as flour. Smells of pencil shavings.
NB never sit on the log in October. Slugs. Also, possibly, the beetle from No. 05.

§ 06 · Soundscapes, written down

What the valley sounded like, eyes shut

I record nothing. Recordings are exact and this is not; these are the shapes of sounds as I remember hearing them.

Dawn · 22 Aprilhazel coppice, 04:40–06:00

Chiffchaff on the two-note metronome; wren detonating underneath it; a song thrush repeating everything three times from the top of the ash as if I might not have caught it the first time. 04:52, first blackbird. Far off, a green woodpecker laughing at something I could not see. By six the whole thing was a wall and I stopped being able to pick out anyone.

Noon · 14 Julythe ford, feet in

Water over stones — a low continuous vowel — and the dry clicking of damselfly wings meeting. A single wood pigeon, bored. Heat has a sound and it is mostly flies. Once, a fish took something off the surface with a noise like a dropped coin.

Dusk · 20 SeptemberOtter Pool, 19:10 onward

Tawny owls warming up: the female's kew-ick, the male's long wavering answer from the hanger. A splash I did not see and have decided was an otter. Bats, audible only as a change in the texture of the quiet. Then the quiet itself, which had a grain to it, like paper.

Night · 17 NovemberKestrel Bank, no moon

Redwings passing overhead in the dark, thin seep calls every few seconds — a river of birds I could hear but not see, arriving from somewhere north and cold. Wind in the last beech leaves like paper being sorted. My coat, loud.

Morning · 14 Decemberbeech hanger, freezing fog

Nearly nothing. Frost cracking somewhere in the litter. A nuthatch's single metallic call, once. My own breath. The best sound of the year was probably this one, and there is almost nothing to write about it.

§ 07 · Supplies

What goes in the bag

  • Hand lens, 10×, on a bootlace round the neck
  • This notebook, plus a waterproof spare for the honest weather
  • Pencils: 2B and 4B, and a knife to sharpen them
  • Dip pen, waterproof ink (freezes at −3°, see January)
  • Small watercolour tin — eight pans, three of them green
  • Waterbrush, and a real brush for when it matters
  • Paper envelopes for spores, feathers, lichen flakes
  • Thermometer, cheap. String, invaluable.
  • Field guides: fungi, mosses, birds by ear (left at home again)
  • Flask. Tea. Non-negotiable.
  • Something to sit on that is not a log (see § 04)
§ 08 · About the naturalist

Who is holding the pencil

self, by the third bench, in pencil because the ink was frozen

Ida Marren

I trained as a printmaker, not a biologist, and it shows: I will always draw the thing before I look it up, and the identifications in this notebook are honest attempts rather than verdicts. I have lived at the top of this valley for eleven years and only started paying proper attention in the twelfth.

The valley is real. Its names here are mine, and slightly wrong on purpose. The third bench on Kestrel Bank is where I sit most mornings; if you find me there, I will happily be corrected about lichens.

  1. Draw it before you look it up.
  2. Write the weather first.
  3. Never take more than you can draw in an hour.
  4. Sit still longer than is comfortable. Then a bit longer.

How the weather is made

One drum at a time

A risograph prints one colour per pass. Every sheet goes round again for the next drum — and lands a little bit off. That wobble is the weather.

The separations cycle on their own. Pick a drum to hold it there for a moment.

Frequently asked

Questions

What is a risograph, anyway?

A risograph is a stencil duplicator from the 1980s — think screenprinting inside a photocopier. It burns a master for each colour, wraps it round a drum full of soy-based ink and pushes paper through one colour at a time. Bright, cheap, slightly unpredictable, deeply loveable.

Do I need a separate ticket for workshops?

Yes. Workshops have 12 seats and are ticketed separately (£18–£22, materials included). Talks, the print swap, kids' stamping and the portfolio clinic are all free with a day or weekend pass.

Is the venue accessible?

Step-free throughout, with a lift to the mezzanine, accessible toilets and a quiet room in the Riso Garage on Sunday. Saturday talks are BSL interpreted. Carers go free — just ask at the desk or email us beforehand for anything specific.

Can I bring my own prints to sell?

Stalls for 2026 are full, but bring prints to Saturday's Great Print Swap, and there's a communal misprint table where anyone can leave work (£3 each, proceeds to the studio fund). The open call for 2027 stalls runs every January.

Cash or card?

Both. Most stalls take card; the Wet Ink Bar is card-only. The nearest cash machine is at the station, six minutes away, so plan your poster budget accordingly.

Are dogs allowed?

Calm dogs on leads are welcome in the Main Hall and the bar. Not in the Workshop Room — wet ink and wagging tails have a history.

Will my prints be dry when I take them home?

Mostly. Riso ink never fully cures, so carry prints flat and interleave them with the free scrap paper at the info desk. A little offsetting is the festival's parting gift.

Still wondering? Write to hello@inkweather.example — a human printer replies within two drying cycles.

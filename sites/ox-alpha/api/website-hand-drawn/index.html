Seasonal Field Entries

March – May

Spring

The stream wakes loud. Wood anemones open by the second week; a chiffchaff repeats its two-note argument from the alder. Found frogspawn in the shallow pool — three clumps, no more than last year.

June – August

Summer

Heat sits in the canopy like a held breath. Dragonflies patrol the riffles; I counted eleven species of bee on one bramble patch in an hour. The moss goes crisp and waits.

September – November

Autumn

The fungus year proper. Chanterelles under beech like dropped coins; a death cap, grim and beautiful, logged but not touched. Redwings arrive on the 14th, right on schedule.

December – February

Winter

Quiet, but never empty. Wren scolds from the bramble; fox prints stitch the frozen puddle margin. Best discovery: a hibernating pipistrelle behind loose bark, breathing once every six seconds.

the long entry · october 9th

An Hour with the Heron

I reached the willow brook before seven, when the mist still owned the water. The heron was already there, of course — grey as the morning itself, folded into the shallows below the second bend like something the river had grown overnight. I sat on the sitting log and did what the best hours demand: nothing.

For twenty minutes it did not move. Not statue-still — statue-still is a lie we tell — but still the way a held breath is still, every feather listening. Then the strike: faster than thought, a spear of neck, and up came a silver flicker that was gone in two tilts of the head. The whole transaction took less than a second and felt like watching a sentence written in ink you can't see until it's finished.

Patience, it turns out, is not waiting. It is attention with nowhere else to be.

It took four fish in my hour. Between catches it stalked with absurd delicacy, one lifted foot hovering mid-air for whole seconds, a dancer who had decided never to finish the step. A moorhen scolded it once from the reeds and was ignored with magnificent completeness. When the sun finally burned through, the heron simply unfolded — wings wider than I remembered, slower than seemed possible — and rowed away upstream, legs trailing like the last stroke of a signature.

I stayed another half hour after it left, which tells you everything about who the hour really belonged to.

About the Naturalist

I am an amateur in the oldest sense — someone who does this for love, unpaid and unhurried. By trade I mend clocks; by calling I sit on logs and write down what the woods are doing. This notebook is my fourth. The first three live in a drawer and smell gloriously of leaf mould.

I believe small things repay attention disproportionately: a moss capsule, a beetle's itinerary, the exact blue of a jay's feather. None of it matters, and all of it matters, and holding both truths at once is the whole practice.

— E. Marsh

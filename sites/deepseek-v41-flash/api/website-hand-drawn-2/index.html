No. 01

Four Seasons, Kept Faithfully

field entries, in order, imperfect

Fig. 2 — fiddleheads, wet hollow, 14 March

14 March · 6°C · damp and close, wind from the west

Spring

The Thaw in which nothing happens, loudly

Six degrees by mid-afternoon and the snow going soft at the edges, the way sugar goes soft when you breathe on it. The mill stream has come over its bank in two places, and where it has, the water is carrying last year's leaves off to somewhere I can't follow.

Skunk cabbage in the wet hollow — three of them, pushing up through the last of the ice with that stubborn, unlovely confidence. I got the first one wrong and drew the spathe too narrow, then sat with it for twenty minutes until the pencil caught up with the plant.

At dusk, the frogs. Not the full chorus yet — just a dozen or so testing the idea. The sound is brown, somehow. It has no shine on it at all.

↳ first frog heard at 18:20, from the boggy meadow. Wrote the time on my cuff, then lost the cuff.

21 June · 23°C · still, midges, sun until 21:14

Summer

The Canopy Closes a green light, underwater

The wood has shut its roof. Under the beeches there's a light that makes everything look as though it's being seen through a hand, and the moss — which I have been measuring weekly since March — has grown eleven millimetres on the north-facing log. Eleven. I have checked it twice.

Up at 04:40 for the warbler count. Forty-one birds, six species, one argument. The wood wren started before there was any light at all and kept going for nineteen minutes without, as far as I could tell, taking a breath.

The stream is low for June. The pool behind the alder roots has gone clear enough to see the bottom, which is mostly caddisfly cases and a very patient stone loach that has been in the same place since April.

9 October · 14°C · low sun, ground wet, smell of leaf-mould

Autumn

Sporefall the ground gets generous

The chanterelles came after the rain and they came everywhere — under the beeches, in the ditch by the quarry gate, three of them in the middle of the path where nobody walks but me and one very interested dog.

Fly agaric at the birch edge, six, all showy. I know they're the obvious one, the one everybody draws. I drew them anyway. The cap is a better red than any red I own; the nearest pan is a lie and I've written that in the corner.

Leaf-fall is late this year. The birch went over in about forty hours, all at once, and the stream has been carrying gold for a week. The light down there at four o'clock is the best thing in the whole notebook, and I say that knowing what's on page ninety.

↳ spore print taken, 10 Oct — still drying on the shed shelf, under the jar.

27 January · 3°C · hard blue sky, no wind at all

Winter

The Quiet Ledger the loudest part is the leaving

Frost on the inside of the window this morning, which means the shed is colder than I thought. Went out at seven with the cracked thermometer and the flask and got the best hour of the year for nothing at all — no birds, no wind, no sound but the ice.

Eight deer crossed the top field in the night. I've followed the tracks up to the gate and drawn them out, though the print in the middle is not a deer and I can't place it. Fox, maybe. A big fox.

The pool has gone to a low grey that stops being water and becomes a kind of metal. The heron stands at the edge of it for fifteen minutes and moves nothing except one eye. Then she leaves, and the leaving is the loudest part of the day.

No. 04 — the long entry

Twenty-Two Minutes at the Heron Pool

3 November · 06:12 until 06:34 · transcript of a page

Fig. 12 — heron, stock still, 06:40

↳ she has been here every morning this week. I have started arriving early, which the dog resents.

The heron arrived at 06:12 and stayed until 06:34, and in all that time I do not think she moved more than a hand's width, unless you count the eye.

Weather3°C, no wind, pool flat LightGrey until 06:31 ResultOne small flat fish

I was on the low bench behind the alders with the flask and a page I had already ruined. The pool at that hour is not really a colour; it is a grey that holds light without returning it. She came in over the mill roof, low, with two heavy beats and a glide, and she put her feet down in the shallow end with the delicacy of someone setting down a full cup.

What followed was mostly waiting. I have written nothing happened in this notebook perhaps sixty times, and it is almost never true.

She arranged herself — one leg, then the other, then a small adjustment of the shoulder that made the whole bird a foot taller. Her head went down into the collar. She became, as she always does, a stick, a shadow, a piece of the bank that happens to be standing up.

“I have written nothing happened in this notebook perhaps sixty times, and it is almost never true.”

At 06:19 a rudd came up under the alder roots and turned, and the heron's head was suddenly not in her collar but out in front of her, level, aimed. She didn't strike. The fish turned again and went back under the roots, and she lifted her head, shook it very slightly, and resumed being a stick.

At 06:26 two moorhens came out of the reeds and quarrelled about something I couldn't see and wouldn't have understood if I could. She paid them nothing at all. At 06:31 the light came up properly off the water and onto the bank, and the whole pool went from grey to a pale, cold, unmistakable green — the colour of the inside of a mussel.

At 06:33 she took one step. One. And speared something in the shallows faster than I can write it. A small flat fish. She turned it twice, worked it down, and stood still again with her shoulders up.

She left at 06:34. Three beats to clear the alders. I stayed another twenty minutes with the flask going cold in my hand. The drawings I have of her on the next four pages are all bad, and the note I made at the time says only this:

level — one leg — still — gone.

— E. M., 3 November

No. 06

What the Hollow Sounds Like

written down within a minute, while it was still happening

  • Dawn chorus, Wren Wood 04:52

    One wood wren starts in the dark and holds the whole thing up like a tent pole. Twenty seconds later the mistle thrush arrives underneath it, and the whole wood leans into the sound. It stops as fast as a curtain in a draught.

  • The mill stream at the ford 11:20

    Under the noise there is a lower note — the stones turning over the fall. If you put your ear to the rail it goes right through your jaw. I do this most mornings and I have no explanation for it.

  • Rain on the beech canopy 15:40

    Not the rain itself — the second sound, made by leaves giving it back to the ground after the shower has gone. Under the beeches you stay dry for four minutes longer than anywhere else, and then all at once you don't.

  • Heron leaving the water 06:34

    Three beats, and the third one is smaller than the first. There is a wet slap, then a pause, then the alder tops. That is the whole event. It is the loudest thing that happens before nine o'clock.

  • Crickets, boggy meadow 21:10

    Stopped by three degrees of drop. One of them keeps going a while after the others, and there is a second of nothing at the end, and that second is the sound I remember.

  • Owl, after midnight 00:47

    Three notes from the alders and then a long gap long enough that I sat up. When it came back it was further down the stream, and it travelled without ever seeming to move.

No. 08 — about

The Naturalist

Elowen Marsh has lived at 4 Mill Lane since 2011, in a house that is colder than the shed and has a considerably worse view of the water. She keeps this notebook for nobody in particular, though the Riverbend Field Society has lately asked for the weather pages, which came as a surprise to everyone including the Field Society.

She is not a scientist. She has a ten-times hand lens, a field guide held together with tape, and a bad habit of drawing things before she has properly understood what she is looking at — which is, she suspects, also the reason she keeps looking.

The rest of the year is in the box under the stairs: fourteen notebooks, some of them wet, one of them chewed. What she would like said about this volume is only that the pages are in order and the dates are true.

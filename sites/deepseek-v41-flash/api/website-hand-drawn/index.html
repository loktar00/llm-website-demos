I.

Spring

14 March — 30 May

The wood is still wearing last autumn. Brown leaf-litter, brown water, brown light through bare hazel. I walk the same loop four mornings running and write nothing changed three times, which is a lie, because on the fourth morning the celandine is out — a scatter of small yellow coins along the ditch where the snow lay longest, closed against the wind and opening the instant the sun touches them.

Everything after that happens too fast to record properly. The willow goes from grey to a soft green fuzz in nine days. The chiffchaff arrives on the 29th and does not shut up until August. Under the board by the stile there is a toad, and has been a toad for at least three weeks; I know from the soft purring sound, low and almost felt rather than heard, made in the leaf mould before it is made anywhere visible.

toad! heard before seen — purring from under the loose board.

Lesser celandine, ditch by the stile — 21 March

II.

Summer

1 June — 20 August

Hayfield heat. The meadow goes from green to a pale dusty blond in nine days, and the air above it becomes a substance — thick with grass pollen and the wings of things. Walking through it in the middle of the afternoon is like walking through warm soup with a smell. You can hear the field before you see it: a dry, continuous rustle, and under that a low hum at about the pitch of a fridge.

The evenings are the good part. On the 12th of July the first meadow browns settled on the warm gate and refused to move for the camera I don't have. On the 24th, at twenty past nine, three elephant hawk-moths came to the buddleia and stayed for eleven minutes, hovering so steadily that I could draw the outline of a wing and get it right. The next morning the swallow chicks on the barn beam had gone.

hawk-moths at dusk, drinking at the buddleia — 21:20, warm, still.

Elephant hawk-moth, buddleia by the barn — 24 July

III.

Autumn

21 August — 31 October

Bolete rain. Four days of it, warm and steady and completely without drama, and on the fifth the beech litter opens into a small city of caps. I counted forty-one in a strip nine paces long. Most were the plain brown ones I still argue with myself about; three were the ceps I had been half-hoping for since the spring, sitting in the leaves like something left out of a pocket.

By the middle of October the wood is at its best and worst at the same time — best in that everything is legible, worst in that there is too much of it. The jays are working the mast from before first light. Under the big oak on the boundary I found a feather and carried it home flat in the notebook, which is how page 118 got its blue stain. The chanterelle ground above Falls Stream produced two dozen over a fortnight. I will not write down where, exactly, and hope that the person reading this understands why.

chanterelle ground — keep quiet about this one.

Ceps & brown caps, beech litter — 3 October

IV.

Winter

1 November — 29 November

Frost ledgers. The pond takes its first skin of ice on the 12th, thin enough to hold the shape of a reed in it and thin enough that a stone makes a sound like a dropped plate. I spent an hour on the bank with my hands in my pockets, listening to the ice make its own weather — a long groan, then a tick, then nothing for four minutes.

The bog moss at the spring head is the only green left in that part of the marsh, and it is a very good green, almost luminous against the dead reed. Squeezed, it gives up water the colour of weak tea. This is the last entry before the hard frost, and the notebook is three-quarters full, which is roughly as much as I expected. The owl was at the top of the ash at 16:40, three notes and then nothing, and I walked home in the dark without needing the torch once.

owl at 16:40 — three notes, then nothing.

First ice, south pond — 12 November

VII.

The Nightjar at Hollow Ridge

6 July · 21:40 – 23:55 · clear, 17°C falling to 11°C · moon two days past new

the copper light on the ridge — couldn't get the colour

I went up to Hollow Ridge to watch the light go out of the heath. The sun had already gone behind the western edge of the plantation, and the top of the ridge held a colour I don't have a good word for — a dull copper, like the underside of a kettle. There was no wind at all. The grass was warm through my trousers and I sat down in it without thinking about whether anything was living there.

For twenty minutes nothing happened, which is the first thing you have to accept about this kind of work. I made a few notes I didn't need. A wood pigeon went over, high and north, and the sound of its wings arrived a moment after it had already gone. Two cockchafers blundered into my sleeve, worked out that it wasn't a tree, and left again. I wrote 21:52, nothing and underlined it, which I do when I'm bored.

Then, at 22:06, the churring started.

If you have not heard a nightjar you will probably be expecting something bird-like. It isn't. It is a low, mechanical purr, at roughly the pitch of an engine left idling in the next field, and it goes on and on and on — a continuous roll that lifts very slightly at the end and then begins again, without any pause you could fit a breath into. It came from a birch on the far side of the ridge. My first thought was that I had misjudged the distance. It sounded forty metres away. It was seven.

I had the torch but I didn't use it. Instead I sat still and let my eyes come up, which takes about a quarter of an hour and cannot be hurried. The heath resolved itself slowly: bracken first, then the darker clumps of heather, then the pale trunks, then the whole geometry of the ridge laid out like something being remembered. Somewhere out on the left a young rabbit moved, thought better of it, and stopped. The churring went on. It is not a sound that fills a place; it is a sound that sits in it.

At 22:40 the churring stopped. This is the part I want to write down properly.

A nightjar in flight is not a bird so much as a decision the air makes and then unmakes.

It came up from the birch without any visible effort and crossed the gap in front of me at about four metres, and in the last of the light it was a grey-brown outline with two white patches near the wingtips and a white flash at the tail, and that was all — no wing-beat you could count, no sound whatsoever. It went down the ridge, turned, and came back. Twice. Three times. Somewhere in there the clapping began: a single hard clap, sharp as a hand on a table, at the top of a rise. Then another. Then four in a row, faster, and then the whole ridge seemed to be doing it, which of course it wasn't — there was one bird, and I had turned it into a crowd.

I have read that the clap is mechanical, made by the wings, that it costs the bird something. Sitting there, the only thing I could think was how much it sounded like the noise a book makes when you shut it too quickly. Something being concluded.

By 23:20 the moon had moved enough to change the ridge from grey to a soft, faint gold, and the moths started coming to the sheet I'd pegged out on the far side. Mostly hundred-and-something species I can't name, plus one large, slightly insulted-looking privet hawk-moth that stayed for a quarter of an hour and let me get a wing drawn properly. A bat worked the line of the birch scrub, back and forth, for as long as I could be bothered to follow it.

I walked down at 23:55 with the torch off. Near the bottom, where the path crosses the little culvert, a hedgehog was doing its slow, unhurried work among the leaves. It stopped when it heard me, then carried on, apparently having satisfied itself that I was not worth the trouble. I have thought about that hedgehog more than I have thought about the nightjar.

Noted for next time: come earlier, bring the second flask, sit further up the ridge, and stop trying to see the bird. It shows itself on its own terms and not before, and the whole pleasure is in being there when it decides to.

— E.V., written up the following morning, still in the same shirt.

IX.

What the place sounds like

Four recordings I didn't make. Written down instead, at the time, which is slower and probably better.

Dawn — Long Field

04:50, 19 May

Starts with one blackbird, always in the same hawthorn, and he isn't so much singing as clearing his throat. Then the robin — thin, slightly apologetic. Then, somewhere between 04:58 and 05:04, the whole hedge comes in at once, like a room filling up with people arriving.

Dusk — South Pond

21:15, 3 August

Three things, mostly: the reed warbler's rattle, the plop of a coot, and something large and entirely uninterested moving in the mud at the far bank. When the light goes, the bats start and the whole pond gets a thin high line drawn over it.

After rain — Blackthorn Wood

14:20, 9 August

Every leaf is a small percussion instrument. Three layers: drip from the canopy (slow, hollow, irregular); the hiss of water arriving in the leaf litter (steady); and underneath both, the stream — the only sound that was there before the rain began and will be there after everything dries.

Night — the heath

23:00, 6 July

Almost nothing. A dog on the far side of the valley, twice. The click of something small in the grass. And then the churring, which is not really a sound of this world's economy — it's the noise a piece of machinery would make if the machinery had decided to live outdoors.

XI.

About the naturalist

Elspeth Vane is a naturalist and illustrator who has lived at the edge of Hollow Ridge for eleven years, in a cottage with a mossy roof and a pump that fails every January. She has never owned a proper camera and considers this a matter of principle rather than budget, though it is also budget.

She keeps four notebooks going at once — one for weather, one for birds, one for fungi, and this one, which is meant to be all of them and has turned into something else. She writes up her observations the following morning, always, because she found out early that memory tidies things and tidying is the enemy of accuracy.

"The trick is not to look harder. It is to stay longer than you want to. Everything interesting happens in the twenty minutes after you have decided to go home."

Elspeth Vane Naturalist & illustrator · The Cottage, Marsh Lane

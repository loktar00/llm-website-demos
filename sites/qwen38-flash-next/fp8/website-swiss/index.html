§ A

Introduction to the Collection

The archive collects the invisible infrastructure of the schedule: the map that promises a route, the sign that ends a doubt, the ticket that measures trust, the clock that obliges us all to the same second. None of these objects are decorations. Each is an argument that a city can be legible.

Acquisitions follow one criterion: the object must have attempted to inform a stranger, quickly, under stress, in any language. The railway clock is the archive's emblem because it makes a moral claim — that the shared timetable is a form of equality.

Study rooms open Tuesday–Sunday. Objects are catalogued by system, medium, and date of first public display. Colour is recorded to spectral standard; typography is catalogued by cut and year.

Light line, museum district. Closed Mondays — a fact the map politely omits.

Caption

Reconstruction after the 1967 diagram (HTA·1967·012). Geometry restricted to horizontal, vertical, and 45° segments; every bend lies on the 40 mm modular grid. Interchange rings are drawn at a legibility floor of 9 px regardless of scale.

Chronology of Transit Information

1897
The Timetable Becomes Law
Standardised continental time binds timetables to a shared clock. The station clock becomes the first mass-produced instrument of civic synchronisation.
Origin
1933
Beck's Diagonal Argument
Geography is discarded; topology wins. The diagram proposes that a map's duty is comprehension, not fidelity. The archive holds 214 derivatives.
Obj. 01

Mechanical Legibility
Split-flap and dot-matrix boards make information theatrical — information with a sound. The board is a machine performing the schedule.

The Pictogram Settlement
Olympic-era symbol systems standardise the language of wayfinding. Strangers across languages agree on the meaning of a walking figure.
Obj. 04
1987
Colour as Code
Line-colour semantics formalised across operators. A hue becomes a route; contrast ratios enter the building code alongside the exit sign.
Study

The Ticket Goes Invisible
Magnetic and smart media retire the paper object. The archive's task shifts from collecting the ticket to documenting its protocol.

Open Data, Closed Fonts
Real-time feeds make the timetable liquid. The museum begins collecting APIs as artefacts — accession numbers assigned to endpoints.

§ E

Featured Designer — E. M. Ruesch

«A good sign is an apology nobody notices — it admits the city got you lost, and fixes it in one second.»

Erika Marlene Ruesch (1929–2011) drew the signets for four provincial networks and never signed a platform. Her office method was severe: walk the line as a stranger, note every hesitation, design only the hesitation away. Sketches were rejected if they required explanation for longer than the time between two stations.

Her 1971 memorandum, held in the archive as HTA·DOC·1971·004, reads like a design system written decades before the term: fixed character heights, a red reserved exclusively for obligation, and the rule that an arrow may never curve more than once. Colour, she insisted, is a contract with colour-blind citizens before it is ever a brand.

Ruesch distrusted novelty in information design with good reason. A sign is useful precisely because it resembles the sign at the last station. Her famous verdict on the redesign of a legacy system remains pinned above the archive's drafting table: «Retire the typeface only when the citizens have emigrated or died.»

The archive preserves 1,140 of her drawings, all grids visible, all corrections left in place — evidence that legibility is an editorial achievement, not a stylistic one.

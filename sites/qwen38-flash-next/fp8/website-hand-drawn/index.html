What the Frozen Stream Kept Winter

The Alder has slowed to a grey thread under its skin of ice. Where the water shallows over the pebble shelf, the frost has drawn perfect ferns across the surface — a whole colony of them growing downward, the only ferns to be found this month. I knelt until my knees complained and copied three.

Empty caddis cases, abandoned like small stone hats on the bed. Something moved along the bank at first light — four-legged, unhurried, gone before I had the pencil out. Only a single print in the mud by the bridge, pointed like a comma.

The Wood Anemones Insist Spring

A hundred at least on the bank below the hornbeam, maybe two hundred — I lost count twice and saw better for it. Each flower tilts its face a different way, as though listening to separate weathers. Beneath, the leaf litter is stitched through with white threads, patient all winter, all now allowed to be leaves.

First chiff-chaff of the year, one clear syllable and then a great deal of opinion about it. The wren in the hazel has begun again also, at full volume, at an hour I decline to record.

A Hoverfly, Being Misunderstood Summer

Sat with a hoverfly on the hogweed for the better part of an hour. It has the waistcoat of a wasp and the manners of nothing at all — no sting, no interest in argument, simply a great hovering and then a full stop, as if rewound. I have worn the same costume theory at dinner parties; it works less well here.

The stream is low and legible. Under the flat stone at the bend: a caddis working, dragging its case backward like a hermit in a very specific tower. We regarded each other. It was too busy.

Buttons of Amber in the Fungi Dell Autumn

The Dell has come fully into its strange season. Deerberries in their hundreds, half of them already going soft and luminous in the leaf cup. One cap, perfect, sunken to the shoulders in beech litter — I photographed nothing and drew it twice, which is the same thing done properly.

Last swallow over the alder carr, single, going southeast with an economy of wing that made my chest ache for reasons I have given up examining.

The Slow Parliament of a Felled Hornbeam

site ④ · observed fortnightly, March to February · one very patient year

ask about this at
the next walk —
the beetle chapter
is the best part

It stands — it stood; the grammar of a stump does not permit the present tense for long — at the fold of the hollow where three fallen leaves agree to collect in every wind. A hornbeam, cut perhaps in the 1970s, left a meter high and silvered by weather into the colour of old bone. I chose it in March for no better reason than that it faced the morning, and I have kept faith with it since, on the theory that a thing watched honestly will eventually explain itself.

In the first month it explained very little: lichens, chiefly. A地图 — no, I had to look the word up in my own mind — a cartography of them. Common candleflame spreading its yellow counties across the north face; the tiny shield-fruits of a star lichening the bark-flakes like punctuation no one has learned to read. I copied what I could and misjudged the rest, which is how copying works.

A stump is not an ending filed under wood. It is a slow parliament in which fungi hold the majority, insects the opposition, and moss simply abstains beautifully.

By May the moss had staged its quiet revolution along the lee edge — not one species but at least three, distinguished by whether the capsules stood upright like penitents or nodded like tired clerks. Under a hand lens borrowed against the dew-point of my own breath, each moss leaf became a lance with a single green nerve, and the whole fringe became suddenly, embarrassing close to a forest, complete with its own weather of held water and vanishing mists that lasted precisely as long as I kept my face near it.

June brought the beetles. A round fungus weevil worked the bark fissures with the patience of a man auditing a ledger; a clerid, wasp-waisted and businesslike, patrolled the same fissures for weevils, and I understood that I had been handed, for the price of an hour's kneeling, the sight of an entire economy doing its Tuesday work. I drew both. The weevil came out better; beetles are honest about geometry, wasps about none of it.

August: the first fungi proper, a fringe of oyster-grey brackets along the base, then the parchment cups of something I have catalogued only as unknown, provisionally hopeful. I collected a spore print on halved sugar lump — brown, the colour of a river after rain — and named nothing, which felt like the first mature thing I had done all year.

October stripped the stump to its line drawing. Frost wrote across its cracks, spiders repaired their anchorages daily with a courtesy toward no one, and one wren used it as a singing post at an hour I decline to record twice in one notebook. I found that I had begun thinking of its decay in the first person plural. This is the hazard of the work and I accept it: you cannot keep a faithful appointment with a dying thing for a year and remain a stranger.

February. Two years and a month old as a subject now. The south rim has gone soft; pinch it and it gives like wet cardboard over dry, and inside there is a dark lattice of galleries, each one somebody's entire biography. The great hornbeam beside it — the standing one, my other acquaintance — has begun to lean a finger of shade across it, and I understand the arrangement: the shade keeps the damp in, the damp keeps the beetles in, the beetles make the softness, and the softness is the whole point. Nothing here is dying in the sense I was raised to mean. It is being redistributed with extraordinary thoroughness and, I would add, tact.

I will come again in March, when it faces the morning, and we will see what the second parliament decides. Bring the 2B pencil; the candleflame is migrating, and the lichens, I now firmly believe, are keeping score of all of us.

05:52 · Dawn Chorus, first movement

A wren, solo, far too loud for its size — the overture always starts on the wrong dynamic. Then robin from the hornbeam, then the whole hedge agreeing in disorder, and underneath it all the stream's grey cymbal, which never stops for applause.

16:40 · Woodpecker, two taps, then argument

Two dry taps from the dead elm — drumming, actually, the six-stroke kind, delivered like a knock on a door one expects to be invited through. Answered, ninety seconds later, from the far carry by an identical knock with a different accent. I have come to read this as: here still mine / and yet, good day.

the pause between taps is the music; do not hurry the page.

21:15 · Dusk, wind in the hornbeam

Ten thousand small paper corrections. The hornbeam leaf is thin and holds its edges stiffly, so the whole tree edits itself long after the birch and alder have gone quiet. Owls take up the far end of the hollow like two parish councils agreeing, and the day's sound retires up-tree, one branch at a time.

The Naturalist

E. Wren

Field naturalist, fourth year in Aldersgate Parish, self-appointed biographer of things too small for the parish newsletter. Trained formally in nothing and informally in everything that moved slowly. The Moss Notebook is volume the first of a promised twelve; volumes are numbered, seasons are not, and accuracy is held to the standard of the pencil rather than the microscope.

I walk the same mile and a half as everyone else and try to notice it differently. Corrections are welcome, especially the kind that arrive on the path itself, in the form of a tugging sleeve and “but have you looked at this.”

Walks run Sundays, nine o'clock, all weathers; the stream waits for no one's umbrella.

— E. Wren
